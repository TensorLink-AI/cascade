    How to read this table

    Each row is a reign: the miner that took the lead, when, and by how much it beat the previous leader in a same-round head-to-head. Miner numbers are network UIDs; Entry links to the exact submitted model; Round is the signed record that awarded the win. Miner UIDs can be re-used by the network over time, so the first-time/returning badges are matched on the miner’s cryptographic key, not the UID.

    How this is measured

    Every round, the subnet tests entries on a fresh set of hidden data. That stops anyone tuning their entry to the test — but it also means a score from round 1 and a score from round 80 were taken on different data and can’t be compared head to head.

    What can be compared is each change of lead: the old leader and the challenger are tested on the exact same data, in the same round. So we record how much better the winner was at each handover, and multiply those steps together. Difficulty of the data cancels out at every step.

    The guaranteed minimum line uses the cautious end of each measurement instead of the best estimate — the same threshold the subnet itself required before awarding the win. Reality sits between the two lines, and the gap is the honest margin of error.

    improvement = 1 − Π(challenger_error ÷ leader_error) across lead changes; the minimum line substitutes each round’s 95% bootstrap lower bound.

    Is improvement becoming more compute-efficient?

    --

    The question this section answers: are we extracting more forecasting accuracy from the same amount of training compute? Accuracy bought with ever more computing power isn’t really progress — so this divides the verified improvement above by the total training the competition has consumed to produce it. The line drifts down through rounds that spend compute without a verified gain, and steps up at each change of lead.

    How does it score on public tests?

    --

    Independent, industry-standard forecasting benchmarks that anyone can run. 1.00 is the standard reference model; below 1.00 beats it.

    Which benchmarks, and why they don’t affect the competition

    GIFT-Eval, BOOM and TIME — three open forecasting benchmarks, scored the same way their public leaderboards do, so the numbers are directly comparable to published results.

    These scores are reported only. They never decide who wins a round. The benchmark data is public, so scoring on it would just reward entries tuned to it; the competition is settled on hidden data instead.

    Does progress carry forward?

    --

    Eventually the best model so far becomes the new starting line for everyone. The question is how much of the accumulated progress survives that handover.
